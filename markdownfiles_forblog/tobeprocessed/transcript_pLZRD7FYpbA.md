The following transcript is based on the audio conversation between an interviewer (Pradip) and a spiritual teacher (Gurudev). The discussion centers on the life of Angulimala, a notorious killer turned Buddhist saint, and parallels with other spiritual transformations.

Pradip: Gurudev, my salutations to you.

Gurudev: Salutations, my son. Speak.

Pradip: Dear viewers, today we will learn about a Buddhist monk who, in his early life, was a mass murderer. Later, he transformed into a saintly monk. Gurudev, from your own mouth, we wish to hear his name and the story of his life.

Gurudev: I will tell you. Many people don’t know this name. His original name was Ahimsaka.

Pradip: And what was he known as later?

Gurudev: I will get to that. First, let everyone know he was Ahimsaka. His father was Mantani and his mother was Bhag-Bhaga. This is his background. His birthplace was Shravasti. He lived during the time of the Buddha. When Buddha was present, within a 20-kilometer radius, no ill deeds took place. People were generally good.

Now, let me tell you his most famous name: Angulimala. At one point, he took initiation from a wicked teacher who pushed him toward the path of violence. He was told to commit acts of violence as a form of practice. He became so extreme that he would go from village to village, cutting off people’s fingers. He would string these fingers together and wear them as a necklace. That is why he was called Angulimala (Anguli meaning finger, Mala meaning necklace).

Small children read this in stories. His story is very similar to that of the robber Ratnakara, who later became Valmiki. Most people know of Ratnakara, but fewer know the details of Angulimala. He used to roam the villages, but he wouldn't loot; his only goal was to cut off fingers to wear as a necklace. He loved wearing that necklace.

He had collected 999 fingers and needed just one more to reach a thousand. He lived on top of a mountain. One day, Buddha was traveling along the path below. Seeing Buddha, Angulimala shouted from the mountain, "I found it! I found another finger!" He rushed down the mountain and stood before Buddha.

He said, "Give me a finger! Then my thousand fingers will be complete." Buddha replied, "You may take it." But Buddha possessed a divine radiance. Angulimala was caught in that glow. He became stunned and speechless. He said, "I don’t want your finger anymore," and he fell at Buddha’s feet.

He told Buddha, "I took initiation from someone by mistake, and I followed what he taught me. Please give me a new initiation." Buddha said, "Fine. Go, bathe, cleanse yourself, and come back. I will initiate you." After the initiation, Buddha told him, "From today, you shall be dedicated to human welfare."

His mind underwent a massive transformation. After 15 or 20 years of meditation and following Buddha’s path, he returned to that same village. Upon seeing him, the villagers began throwing stones and bricks at him. His body was wounded and bleeding, but he didn't say a word. Everyone threw stones, thinking, "He has returned to cut more fingers."

In that state, he returned to Buddha. Buddha asked, "Why are you in this condition?" He explained, "I oppressed people, and they have returned that oppression to me. I have no sorrow. You gave me the path of non-violence, and I have embraced it. I did nothing to them."

Later, his mother was also questioned by the villagers because of her son’s past. During the time he was a killer, the king had ordered him to be imprisoned. But after meeting Buddha, everything changed. Angulimala, out of remorse, went to the houses of those whose fingers he had cut. He faced their stones but remained non-violent.

Slowly, the people he had harmed began coming to him. He eventually initiated many of them. Buddha himself told him he could initiate people. It was an amazing thing—those whose fingers he had cut ended up taking spiritual initiation from him.

Pradip: Gurudev, from what you are saying, it is clear that if a person doesn't find a true Guru, their life can fall into darkness and confusion.

Gurudev: Exactly. Without a true Guru, if one finds a wicked teacher, the result is what happened to him initially. His mother eventually admitted to the king's men and the police what her son had been doing. But through Buddha’s grace, he was brought from the path of violence to the message of peace.

He even built Gompas (monasteries) that still bear his name. He was a dacoit turned monk. As I mentioned, the story of Ratnakara is similar. Ratnakara used to kill people for a living. One day, Narayana (God) himself appeared before him and asked, "Who will share the burden of your sins?" Ratnakara replied, "My wife and children, of course." Narayana said, "Tie me to this tree and go ask them."

Ratnakara went home and asked his parents, and they said, "No, your sins are yours. It is your duty to feed us, but we won't take your sins." He went to his wife, and she said the same: "You married me and brought me here; it is your responsibility to provide, but I won't share your sins."

Hearing this from everyone, he felt deep remorse. He realized that while he was committing the sins, he was alone in the consequences. He returned to Narayana, who told him, "You see? You alone must bear these sins." Ratnakara then began his penance. He meditated so long that an anthill (Valmika) grew over him. Thus, he became known as Valmiki.

Pradip: These stories serve as a great lesson for humanity.

Gurudev: Yes. Whatever actions you perform, you must learn from them. Transformation happens when a person realizes their wrongdoings. When someone is doing bad things, they often don't realize it. But there comes a time when remorse begins. Whether a Guru is there or not, that realization is when God shows a path.

Karma is inescapable. No one—not your wife, not your children—will share the fruits of your sins. You must think: "Am I committing a sin or a virtuous act?" People often don't take the time to think. They often chase away good people because they are too busy with their wrongdoings.

Even Bhishma Pitamah from the Mahabharata had to answer for his past. When he was lying on the bed of arrows, he asked Krishna, "I have committed no sin in this life, so why am I suffering like this?" Krishna told him to look back 100 births. Bhishma looked back and saw that even then he had done nothing wrong. Krishna told him to look even further.

Bhishma eventually saw a past life where he was a King. He was traveling with his soldiers and saw a snake on the road. He ordered his men not to kill it but to move it. One of his men took the snake and threw it into a bush of thorns, where the snake was pierced and suffered. Krishna explained, "You gave the order. As the King, the responsibility is yours. That is why you are suffering on this bed of arrows now."

Pradip: Gurudev, you've given us a powerful example—how someone can be a killer in one phase of life and a saint in the next through the guidance of a true Guru.

Gurudev: I'll give you a modern example. About a year ago, a man came to me from Uttar Pradesh. Let's call him "Munna Bhai." He had committed many murders and killed many animals. He was a hardened criminal. He found me and asked, "Will you initiate me?" I told him, "Give me your gun first, then I will initiate you."

He was shocked. I asked him how many people and animals he had killed. He admitted everything and said, "I want to leave that life. I want to return to being a normal human. I want to stop the violence." I initiated him. He didn't give me the gun—he honored it too much—but he swore in front of the Divine Mother that he would never commit violence again. He stayed here for about a year and recently moved to Gujarat for work.

I saw with my own eyes that a man can change. When the conscience (Vibek) or consciousness (Chaitanya) awakens, a person changes. Once the conscience wakes up, a person has nothing left to do but seek a better path or face the end of their old self.

Pradip: Gurudev, we will conclude here for today. My salutations to you.

Gurudev: Salutations, my son.
