Student: If you could tell our audience a bit more about Mahashashthi...

Guru: Well, Shashthi is a vow (brata) observed by women. They follow it throughout the day. After performing the Shashthi Puja and having Prasad, they break their fast with water. The rule for Durga Shashthi is that the puja happens first. In the morning, there is a puja held under the Bael tree (Belltala). The priest performs the Shashthi Puja, and those offering the puja eat the Prasad and then drink water before eating other food. Since it is a religious vow, they don't eat rice on that day; they eat foods made of flour (maida), like luchi. That is the rule.

The morning puja is completed, and then in the evening, another ritual takes place under the same Bael tree. This is called the Bodhon (Awakening). This is when the Mother Goddess is welcomed. She first arrives in the branch of the Bael tree. On the evening of Shashthi, she enters the Bael branch.

Then, the priests perform the ritual of binding the Nabapatrika (the nine plants). Nine different plants are required for Goddess Durga. These plants are bound together with the trunk of a banana tree using the fiber of the banana plant.

The nine plants and the deities they represent are:

Paddy (Dhanyo): Representing Lakshmi, the goddess of wealth and food.

Bael (Bilva): Representing Shiva.

Turmeric (Haridra): Representing Goddess Durga.

Black Colocasia (Kaal Kochu): Representing Goddess Kali. It is said Kali resides here. Mother Kali enjoys Kochu Saag (colocasia greens); we often eat it on Dashami as a tradition.

Giant Taro (Maankochu): Representing Goddess Chamunda.

Pomegranate (Darimbo): Representing Rakta Dantika. Pomegranate is known to increase blood in the body, which is why it is associated with her.

Ashok: Representing the dispeller of sorrows (Shokrahita). People consume Ashok buds to remain free of grief.

Jayanti: Representing Kartikeya, who is said to reside in this plant.

These nine plants are bound to the banana tree and kept there. In the evening, the Bodhon occurs, where the Mother is invoked. She arrives with her whole family in the Bael branch. This ritual is also known as Nabapatrika Basinyai Durgayai Namah.

Many people mistakenly refer to this as the "Kola Bou" (Banana Bride) because the banana tree is placed next to Ganesha. But in reality, it is the worship of the nine plants as Durga. On Saptami morning, the Nabapatrika is taken for a ritual bath in the Ganges or a pond, accompanied by music and bells.

Student: Gurudev, many people follow different customs, but the cat is known as the vehicle (vahana) of Goddess Shashthi. There are many superstitions regarding cats. Could you clarify this?

Guru: Some people avoid cats due to medical reasons, like the risk of diphtheria from their fur or saliva. However, the cat is the vehicle of Goddess Shashthi for a reason. A cat’s "womb is never empty"—they give birth every few months. Goddess Shashthi is the goddess of progeny and protection of children. She is often depicted with a child in her arms. The cat symbolizes this constant cycle of creation and fertility. While we might have scientific reasons to be cautious of cats, spiritually, the cat is the sacred vehicle of the Goddess who protects our lineage and children.

Guru (continuing on Durga’s arrival): We also look at how the Mother arrives and departs. If she arrives on a horse (ghora) and leaves on a horse, it signifies Morok (pestilence or great mortality). This year, she arrives and departs on a horse. We can see the state of the world—so many people are dying, and more may suffer.

In West Bengal, we don't focus on these nine forms as much as they do in places like Bihar or among non-Bengalis, who observe the nine days of Navratri with great devotion. They worship a different form each day. In Bengal, we mostly observe the Mahalaya and then the main days of the Puja, but the Navadurga worship is a significant part of the wider tradition.

Ultimately, the Shashthi vow is observed by mothers for the well-being and long life of their children. While science offers its own perspectives on things like cats and hygiene, our scriptures provide these symbolic traditions to protect our families and heritage.
