<h2>The Ritual of Shaba Sadhana</h2>

Before the main Kali Puja begins, **Shaba Sadhana** (dead-body worship) must be performed. This is a tradition we have followed for 32 years. A dead body is brought to the pyre, bathed, fed, and offered incense. This is done to please the ghosts, spirits, and companions of the Mother—the eight beings who accompany her—so they do not create obstacles during the worship.

This ritual requires the permission of the deceased's family and the crematorium workers. It is a grueling, hour-long process where each companion of the Mother is worshipped individually. Only after they are satisfied can the main worship begin.

<h2>The Guardians of the Crematorium</h2>

The cremation ground is protected by the **Shoshan-rokkhok** (Protector) and the **Shoshan-dwari** (Gatekeeper). The Protector is a fierce being. If he appears, one must immediately offer a few drops of blood from their own body to the earth; otherwise, death is certain. 

In my early days of practice, I was often accompanied home late at night by two guards provided by the Mother. I warned my family never to look toward the door when I returned, as these guardians would escort me to the entrance before returning to the cremation ground.

<h2>The Necessity of the Bamboo Staff</h2>

During Shaba Sadhana, a second person must always be present, holding a bamboo staff. Spirits are terrified of bamboo. One can never be certain if a dead body might wake up or show movement during the ritual. If it does, alcohol is poured into its mouth, or the bamboo staff is used to settle it. This is a secret and dangerous practice that requires the direct blessing of a Guru.

<h2>The Physical Toll of Secret Worship</h2>

Performing Shaba Sadhana takes an immense toll on the physical body. It is so demanding that one often needs medical attention afterward. I am fortunate to have many skilled doctors as my disciples who understand the nature of this work and look after my health after these intense nights.
